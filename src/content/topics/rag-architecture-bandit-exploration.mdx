Four topics have now chosen a retrieval architecture, and each removed one assumption from the
one before it. The first measured what six named patterns actually buy and where each one fails.
The second showed that the choice resolves only after a workload mixture, a price ratio and an
amortization horizon are fixed. The third unfixed the mixture and made it something you estimate
from traffic. The fourth let it move, and found that most apparent changes are the estimator
rather than the workload.

All four share an assumption that none of them names. Each of them **reads a table**. Quality and
both costs, for six architectures across six kinds of question, measured once and available to
consult. Every argument in the arc is an argument about which row of that table to pick.

A deployment has no such table. It runs one architecture per query and sees how that one did.
The other five entries in every column are counterfactual, and they stay counterfactual — there
is no way to learn what the arm you did not run would have returned on the query you already
answered. The table is not an input to the decision. It is **estimated by the decision**, one
query at a time, and every query spent measuring a bad architecture is a query answered badly.

<RigorFlag>
Nothing about retrieval is re-measured here. The six architectures, their per-query outcomes and
their costs arrive unchanged through the import chain from the four predecessor topics. The only
new object is the **feedback structure** — what a deployment is allowed to observe — and every
claim below is a claim about policies rather than about retrieval.
</RigorFlag>

## What a deployment can and cannot see

The setup carries over intact. Traffic is a mixture over five kinds of question; each
architecture $a$ has a quality on each kind and two costs, netted at fixed prices into a utility
$u(a, w)$ that is affine in the workload $w$. The predecessor's drift path carries the bridge
share from $0.20$ to $0.70$ over the deployment, and the architecture that is genuinely best
changes from `hybrid` to `agentic` on the way.

What is new is the observation model. At query $t$ the deployment commits to one architecture
$a_t$, and observes

$$
r_t = Q(a_t, q_t) - \lambda\big[\mathrm{ops}(a_t, q_t) + \rho\,\mathrm{calls}(a_t, q_t)\big]
$$

and nothing else. The full outcome table for this corpus is $6 \times 100$; a deployment of
$2{,}400$ queries collects $2{,}400$ observations out of the $14{,}400$ outcomes that exist — one
in six — and the five-sixths it misses are exactly the ones that would say whether the arm it ran
was the right one.

Regret is measured against the best **fixed** architecture in hindsight, which is the object the
whole arc has been optimizing:

$$
R_T = \max_{a} \sum_{t=1}^{T} u(a, w_t) \; - \; \sum_{t=1}^{T} u(a_t, w_t) .
$$

<FinanceCaseStudy>
The practical shape of this is a team that can run one retrieval stack in production. They can
read a benchmark, but a benchmark is someone else's traffic; what their own traffic rewards is
knowable only by serving it. Every week spent running a configuration to find out what it is
worth is a week of answers produced by a configuration that may be the wrong one, and the bill
for finding out arrives whether or not the answer is useful.
</FinanceCaseStudy>

## An exploring policy beats the best fixed arm

Four policies on the same deployments, averaged over $24$ of them:

| policy | regret | spread | switches | beats the best fixed arm |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| greedy | 123.1 | 143.4 | 16.2 | 1 / 24 |
| constant margin $\delta = 0.10$ | 123.5 | 117.3 | 9.0 | 0 / 24 |
| Thompson sampling | 42.4 | 47.7 | 1517.5 | 4 / 24 |
| **UCB** | **−93.2** | **27.8** | 396.6 | **24 / 24** |

The last row is negative, on every deployment. An uncertainty-weighted policy does not approach
the best fixed architecture — it beats it outright.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="This is a weak benchmark, not a beaten oracle">
Negative regret against the best arm in hindsight sounds like a contradiction and is not one. The
best *fixed* arm is the best single choice held for the whole deployment, and the workload drifts
across a boundary partway through: `hybrid` is right early, `agentic` is right late, and no single
row is right throughout. Choosing the best arm at every moment instead would have been worth
$929.6$ more than the best fixed arm collects — and a policy that keeps measuring collects part of
that gap simply by following the change.

The uncomfortable implication runs backwards through the arc. Every previous topic optimized a
fixed-arm deployment, carefully, under progressively weaker assumptions — and the benchmark they
were all optimizing is one that a policy with no table at all can beat, provided it keeps looking.
</TheoremBlock>

## The previous topic's instrument stops resolving

The fourth topic's whole apparatus was a scalar: how much better must a challenger look before
the incumbent is displaced? It found a clean interior optimum in that parameter, with both ends of
the range losing on every seed.

Applied here, with the table unknown, the same sweep gives:

| margin $\delta$ | regret | standard error |
|---|---|---|
| −0.20 | 136.4 | 26.6 |
| −0.12 | 119.4 | 25.4 |
| −0.08 | 115.7 | 25.6 |
| **−0.05** | **112.4** | 25.7 |
| 0.00 | 123.1 | 29.3 |
| +0.10 | 123.5 | 23.9 |
| +0.20 | 171.3 | 37.6 |

**Ten of the twelve** settings sit within one standard error of the best of them. There is no
longer an optimum to find.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="A constant cannot express what is worth knowing">
The natural guess is that the optimal margin has moved, or changed sign — that where hysteresis
once counselled patience, ignorance now counsels restlessness. The measurement does not support
that, and the reason is structural rather than numerical.

In the previous topic every arm's estimated utility refreshed every window, because utility was a
known function of an observed workload. A margin could therefore compare two numbers that were
equally fresh. Here an arm's estimate refreshes only when that arm is run, so the comparison is
between one well-measured incumbent and five numbers of wildly varying quality — and a *constant*
margin has no way to say which is which. The quantity that matters is not how much better a
challenger looks but how confident you are in the comparison, and no single number carries that.

It shows up as unreliability as much as error: the margin rule's spread across deployments is
$4.53\times$ that of an uncertainty-weighted policy.
</TheoremBlock>

Paired against UCB on the same deployments — which cancels the shared difficulty of a traffic
draw, since some draws are simply harder than others — the separations are unambiguous: greedy
$+216.3$ at $p = 3.1 \times 10^{-7}$, the constant margin $+216.6$ at $p = 1.3 \times 10^{-8}$,
Thompson $+135.5$ at $p = 1.9 \times 10^{-12}$.

One comparison in that table does **not** separate. A sliding window is the standard remedy for a
bandit whose target moves, and it is directionally better here — $-7.9$ against plain UCB — but at
$p = 0.19$ it does not clear the noise. On a single slow changeover, a policy whose confidence
intervals are already wide enough to keep sampling every arm does not need to be told to forget.
We report that rather than explaining it away; a faster or repeated drift would very likely change
it, and that is a different corpus.

## A switching cost is a tax on exploration

Exploration means running an architecture you do not currently believe in, and in this arc
changing architecture costs something real. Total cost is regret plus the price of every switch,
and because a policy's trajectory does not depend on that price, the whole sweep is arithmetic on
one simulation:

$$
\mathrm{total}(p) = R_T + p \cdot \#\{\text{switches}\} .
$$

The switch counts are what decide it. A rule that mostly holds still switches $16.2$ times; UCB
switches $396.6$; Thompson sampling switches $1{,}517.5$. The tax is levied per switch, so it is
levied on exploration in direct proportion to how much of it a policy does.

At a price of $0.56$ per switch the ordering flips: past that point the cheap rule that rarely
moves wins, not because it has learned anything but because it has stopped paying to find out.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="Ignorance as an optimal policy">
That crossover is the honest end of the arc's cost reasoning. The previous four topics treated
information as free and choice as expensive. Here information is the thing being bought, the
price is per switch, and above a threshold the rational move is to stop buying — to hold an
architecture you are not confident in, because confirming your doubt costs more than the doubt
does. It is worth noticing which policy wins there: not the best-informed one, but the one that
gave up on being informed.
</TheoremBlock>

## Why the famous rate cannot be read off this corpus

Bandit regret is usually quoted as $\sqrt{T}$, and under a switching cost as $T^{2/3}$ — a
genuinely striking result, since it says a switching cost changes the *exponent* rather than the
constant. It is tempting to look for that exponent in a simulation, and the temptation should be
resisted here.

| horizon $T$ | UCB regret |
|---|---|
| 300 | 23.7 |
| 600 | 36.1 |
| 1200 | 54.3 |
| 2400 | 71.8 |
| 4800 | 83.8 |

Regret against $\log T$ is linear with $R^2 = 0.995$. That is the instance-dependent rate: on a
corpus with a fixed gap between the arms, UCB's regret is logarithmic, which is the Lai–Robbins
result and is as good as any policy can do.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="A power law fitted to a logarithm">
Fit a power law to those same five points anyway and it returns a slope of $0.464$ — close enough
to $\tfrac{1}{2}$ to read as a confirmation of the $\sqrt{T}$ rate, and it confirms nothing. It is
a power law fitted to a logarithm over less than two decades, and it would have looked just as
convincing had the true behavior been anything else smooth.

The rates are **minimax**: worst cases over instances, where the gap between arms is allowed to
shrink with the horizon so the problem stays hard as $T$ grows. A fixed corpus has one fixed gap.
No amount of simulating it at larger $T$ will produce those exponents, because the quantity they
describe is a maximum over problems and this is one problem.
</TheoremBlock>

## What this establishes, and what it does not

On this instance: an uncertainty-weighted policy beats the best fixed architecture outright, on
every deployment tested, because drift makes that benchmark weak; a constant margin — the previous
topic's entire instrument — stops resolving once the table must be learned, with ten of twelve
settings indistinguishable and $4.53\times$ the spread of the alternative; and a switching cost
taxes exploration in proportion to how much of it a policy does, with a crossover past which the
least informed rule wins.

What it does not establish is any of the numbers as transferable. The crossover price is in this
arc's utility units at this arc's prices. The reward model is a Bernoulli outcome plus a
deterministic per-class cost, which is cleaner than production traffic and flatters every policy
here. And the drift is a single slow changeover, which is the most forgiving non-stationarity
there is — it is why forgetting did not measurably pay, and a workload that moved faster or
repeatedly would likely reverse that.

The transferable content is the shape. If you cannot say what your alternatives are worth on your
own traffic, that is not a gap in your benchmark; it is a cost you have not yet decided whether to
pay. This topic is a measurement of what it costs and of what declining to pay it leaves behind.

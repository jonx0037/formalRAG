Three topics have now chosen a retrieval architecture, and each removed one assumption from the
one before it. The first measured what six named patterns actually buy and where each one fails.
The second showed that "which should we use?" resolves only after a workload mixture, a price
ratio and an amortization horizon are fixed, and that no architecture wins everywhere. The third
unfixed the mixture: you do not know your workload, you estimate it, and a partition of the
simplex is precisely the structure that turns a small estimation error into a different
recommendation.

One assumption survived all three. Each treated the workload as **static** — unknown, perhaps,
but not moving. It moves. Traffic to a financial retrieval system in October is not the traffic
it saw in June; a new filing season, a new asset class, a change in who is asking, and the
mixture that the architecture was chosen for has drifted out from under it.

Once the workload moves, a second thing becomes true that was invisible before: changing
architecture costs something. Reindexing a corpus under a different scheme, redeploying, and
re-tuning the thresholds are real work, paid once per change rather than once per query. So the
question stops being *which* architecture and becomes **when** to change to it.

That is a sequential decision problem, and its answer has a shape: a **control band**. Hold what
you have until a rival beats it by more than a margin. What this topic is for is the measurement
of what that margin is actually doing, which turns out not to be what the setup suggests.

## The estimate is now a stream

The predecessor's setup carries over intact. A deployment sees traffic made of six kinds of
question, and its workload is the mixture $w$ over those kinds. Each architecture $a$ has a
utility $U(a, w)$ that nets quality against both costs at fixed prices, and — the fact the whole
arc leans on — that utility is *affine* in the workload:

$$
U(a, w) = (M w)_a + c_a .
$$

The predecessor observed traffic once and decided once. Here the deployment lives through $T = 60$
windows. In each window $t$ it observes $40$ queries, forms the empirical mixture $\hat w_t$, and
has to decide whether to move. Writing $\hat U_t(a) = U(a, \hat w_t)$ for what the operator can
see and $U(a, w_t)$ for the truth they are scored against, the policy is:

$$
a_t = \begin{cases}
  \arg\max_a \hat U_t(a) & \text{if } \max_a \hat U_t(a) - \hat U_t(a_{t-1}) > \delta \\
  a_{t-1} & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
$$

with $a_1 = \arg\max_a \hat U_1(a)$. The band $\delta$ is the whole policy. Setting $\delta = 0$
recovers the predecessor's plug-in rule applied afresh at every window — switch whenever the
estimated best architecture changes — and the module checks that identity as a sequence of arms,
not merely as a count.

Regret is scored against the true workload at each window, never against the estimate, so a
policy is charged for its mistakes rather than for its beliefs:

$$
R(\delta) = \frac{1}{T} \sum_{t=1}^{T} \Big[ \max_a U(a, w_t) - U(a_t^\delta, w_t) \Big] .
$$

The workload we send through this is a **designed** input, and worth naming as such: the bridge
share — the fraction of questions whose answer requires following a mention from one filing to
another — drifts linearly from $0.20$ to $0.70$, crossing one boundary of the predecessor's
partition. A designed path makes the phenomenon legible, and it also makes it fair to ask whether
the conclusions are artifacts of a straight line, which is why a path that crosses, retreats and
crosses back is run alongside it throughout.

## A rule that re-decides every window changes its mind twelve times

Along that drifting path, the architecture that is genuinely best changes **once**. A rule that
re-decides from each window's traffic switches **12.3** times, averaged over ten deployments.

That gap is the topic. Eleven of those twelve changes are not responses to the workload, because
the workload only did something once. They are responses to $40$ queries being a small sample.

<FinanceCaseStudy>
What this looks like in a deployment is a team that reindexes the corpus under a new scheme in
March, reverts in April, and reindexes again in May, each time with a defensible reading of that
month's traffic in hand. No one in that story is being careless. Each decision is the best
response to the evidence available at the time, and the evidence is genuinely noisy. This is the
failure mode that is hardest to see from inside, because every individual step survives review.
</FinanceCaseStudy>

The cleanest way to see that this is the estimator and not the workload is to remove the
workload entirely. Hold the mixture perfectly constant — zero drift, for all sixty windows — and
count switches anyway:

| bridge share | real changes | myopic switches |
|---|---|---|
| 0.20 | 0 | 1.8 |
| 0.35 | 0 | 19.3 |
| **0.43** | **0** | **30.8** |
| 0.45 | 0 | 29.5 |
| 0.55 | 0 | 10.3 |
| 0.70 | 0 | 1.0 |

Deep inside a cell, at $0.20$ or $0.70$, the deployment makes its first choice and stops. Near
$0.43$ it changes architecture on roughly half the windows of its life, in response to a workload
that never changed at all. This is the predecessor's result arriving on the time axis: distance
to a cell boundary decided how much an estimation error cost there, and it decides how much a
deployment chatters here. Nothing new is needed to explain it, which is the point.

## The band, and why it is not a trade

The fix is to make the incumbent harder to displace. The classical result behind this is
familiar from inventory control: when changing state costs a fixed amount, the optimal policy is
not a threshold but a **band**, and it is optimal to do nothing inside it. We take that as the
frame and demonstrate the band on this instance rather than deriving its optimality in general.

Sweeping $\delta$ gives:

| band $\delta$ | switches | regret |
|---|---|---|
| 0 | 12.3 | 0.00961 |
| 0.02 | 10.6 | 0.00901 |
| 0.05 | 7.8 | 0.00680 |
| **0.10** | **4.4** | **0.00420** |
| 0.20 | 2.0 | 0.00552 |
| 0.40 | 1.9 | 0.06720 |

The first column behaves as anyone would expect: a wider band switches less. The second column is
the one worth pausing on. Regret does not pay for that stability — it **improves**, from $0.00961$
at $\delta = 0$ to $0.00420$ near $\delta = 0.10$, a reduction of more than half.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="The suppressed switches were wrong, not merely expensive">
If hysteresis were a trade — stability bought with decision quality — then regret would rise
monotonically as the band widened, and the band's value would have to be argued from the
switching cost alone. It does not rise. Over the range where the band is suppressing switches
that the noise proposed, it is removing decisions that were *incorrect*, and so it improves both
columns at once. The trade only begins past the optimum, where the band is wide enough to hold an
architecture in place after it has genuinely been beaten; by $\delta = 0.40$ the policy is nearly
inert and regret is an order of magnitude worse than doing nothing at all.
</TheoremBlock>

Both ends of that range lose. That is asserted seed by seed rather than on the average, because
an interior optimum that exists only in the mean is a weaker object than one that exists in every
run — and because this arc has been burned by a fragile interior optimum before. Across all ten
deployments, the best band is interior every time and beats both the myopic rule and the widest
band every time. Where it sits varies: the argmin lands on $0.02$, $0.05$, $0.10$ and $0.20$
depending on the seed. So the claim the module makes is that an interior optimum **exists**, and
never where it is.

## Hysteresis is about noise, not about cost

Here is the part that inverts the setup. We introduced the band by way of a switching cost, and
the classical result that motivates it is a result about switching costs. So the natural
expectation is that the band is a device for amortizing an expensive change, and that making
changes free should collapse it.

It does not. Sweeping the price $c$ of a single switch, and choosing the band that minimizes
total cost $R(\delta) + c \cdot \#\{\text{switches}\} / T$:

| price of a switch | optimal band | switches | regret |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0** | **0.10** | 4.4 | 0.00420 |
| 0.001 | 0.10 | 4.4 | 0.00420 |
| 0.005 | 0.10 | 4.4 | 0.00420 |
| 0.02 | 0.10 | 4.4 | 0.00420 |
| 0.1 | 0.20 | 2.0 | 0.00552 |

At a price of exactly zero — switching is free, there is nothing to amortize — the optimal band
is already $0.10$, the same band that is optimal across four decades of price. The price only
moves it at $c = 0.1$, where it widens to $0.20$.

That flatness is the evidence. A band whose job was paying for switches would track the price of
switches continuously. This one does not notice the price until the price is large, because its
first job is something else: it is **filtering an estimator**. The band has to be wide enough to
absorb the fluctuation in $\hat U_t$ and no wider, and how wide that is depends on how many
queries each window observes — not on what a reindex costs. The switching cost is a second, and
here a much smaller, reason to keep the band.

This is why the static table above is the topic's control rather than a curiosity. A deployment
whose workload never moves at all still needs a band, and needs the *same* band, because the
thing the band is protecting against was never the workload.

## Detecting is not deciding

There is an obvious objection to all of this: why infer drift from the decision at all? Monitor
the workload directly, alarm when it moves, and switch then.

The drift detectors for exactly this arrive unchanged from the calibration topic — a two-sample
Kolmogorov-Smirnov test and the population stability index, run on a twenty-window reference
against the last twenty windows. Pointing them at the same streams the policy sees:

| path | real changes | myopic | banded | KS $p$ | PSI | the detectors say |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| static | 0 | 35 | 17 | 0.9831 | 0.039 | nothing happened |
| ramp | 1 | 6 | 2 | 0.0000 | 2.569 | drift |
| wobble | 2 | 15 | 5 | 0.8320 | 0.303 | drift (PSI only) |

The two instruments fail on the same data in opposite directions.

On the **static** path the detectors are entirely correct — nothing happened, and they say so at
$p = 0.98$ — and a myopic policy changes architecture $35$ times regardless. Detection is right
and useless, because the problem was never that the workload moved.

On the **wobbling** path the situation reverses. The genuinely best architecture changes twice,
and the policy has to move with it. The Kolmogorov-Smirnov test sees nothing at all, $p = 0.83$,
and it is not wrong either. The excursion happens in the **middle**. The reference window spans
$[0.43, 0.62]$ and the current window $[0.32, 0.67]$, while between them the share falls to
$0.29$ — so the two windows the test compares really are similar, and everything that happened
happened where the test does not look. PSI, comparing the same two windows with less power to say so,
alarms weakly.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="A detector compares two moments; a policy lives through all of them">
This is the structural difference, and it is why one does not substitute for the other. A
reference-against-current test is a statement about two points in time, and it is blind by
construction to everything between them — including a full excursion that returns. A policy has
to be correct at every window it passes through. The two questions are genuinely different:
*did the workload move?* and *should I move?* Drift detection is a monitoring instrument, and a
good one; it is not a switching rule, and the failure modes above are what goes wrong when it is
used as one.
</TheoremBlock>

That contrast is also what keeps this topic distinct from the calibration one it borrows from.
There, drift is detected in retrieval *quality*, and the topic stops at detection — which is the
right place to stop when the response is a human investigation. Here the response is automatic
and expensive, so the question is not whether the signal is real but whether acting on it pays,
and those have different answers often enough to be worth separating.

## What this establishes, and what it does not

The measurements are specific and the framing is general, so it is worth being explicit about
which is which.

What the simulation establishes on this instance: a myopic rule chatters at an order of magnitude
above the true rate of change; a control band suppresses that chatter while *improving* decision
quality rather than trading against it; the best band is interior on every deployment tested; and
it is already wide when switching is free, which locates its purpose in the estimator rather than
in the switching cost. The non-monotone path reaches the same verdict on every one of those
points, so none of them is an artifact of a straight-line drift.

What it does not establish: that $0.10$ is a band anyone else should use. The optimal band depends
on how many queries a window observes, on how far the traffic sits from a cell boundary, and on
the prices at which the utility was computed — all of which are held fixed here at one arc's
values. The transferable content is the shape of the answer and the diagnostic that goes with it.
If a deployment is changing architecture far more often than its workload is changing, the band
is too narrow, and the right response is to widen it rather than to improve the estimate.

The classical control-band result is cited as the frame, not re-derived: what is shown here is
that a band is better than a threshold on this problem, not that the optimal policy for this
problem is a band. The observation model is also the most favorable one available to a detector —
a clean multinomial per window, independent across windows — and real traffic is autocorrelated,
which makes every detector in the final movement look better here than it would in production.
The gap that movement measures is therefore a lower bound on the gap between detecting and
deciding, which is the direction that keeps its conclusion safe.

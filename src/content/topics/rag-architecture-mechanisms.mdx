## The missing column

Every survey of RAG architectures is a table. Down the left, the names: naive, hybrid, HyDE,
corrective, GraphRAG, agentic. Across the top, one column — *best for*. Hybrid is best for queries
mixing keywords and concepts. GraphRAG is best for global questions. Agentic is best for
multi-step reasoning.

There is never a second column, and the second column is the one that decides anything. *Best for*
tells us where an architecture succeeds; it does not tell us what it costs where it does not, and
an engineer choosing among six patterns needs both. A pattern that wins one class of query and is
merely expensive elsewhere is a different proposition from one that wins that class and actively
damages another.

So we will supply the missing column. We take six architectures, implement each as an *arm* with a
uniform interface, and run all six over the same queries — measuring not only the class each one
wins but the class each one loses, and attributing both to a named property of the corpus. The
thesis we will end up defending is stronger than "it depends":

> No arm wins both regimes, and every win is attributable to a property of the corpus rather than
> to the architecture being better. An architecture is a bet about where the answer lives.

## Two places an answer can live

An architecture can only be right about a corpus. To make that concrete we build two, and run
every arm on both.

In the **local regime** the answer is carried by a passage. Some filing says the thing; the job is
to find it. This is factoid retrieval, and it is what nearly every retrieval benchmark measures.

In the **global regime** the answer is carried by no passage at all. The question is *which sector
discusses this theme most*, and the answer is a count across a community — a property of a set of
documents that no single document contains. A corpus can be exhaustively indexed, perfectly
embedded, and still not have the answer written down anywhere in it.

The global regime is easy to build badly, and the bad build is worth naming because it is the
default. If the sector that discusses a theme most were also the sector geometrically nearest that
theme, then ranking entities by similarity would already answer the question and community
summarization would be decoration. We therefore *decouple the count from the geometry*: each theme
sits nearest one sector while a different sector holds the most mentioning companies. This is not
a detail, it is the whole regime, so a test asserts it — on all sixteen themes the count-maximizing
sector differs from the nearest one, and flat top-ten voting scores one out of sixteen, which is
*worse than guessing* among five sectors.

<TheoremBlock type="definition" number={1} title="The two regimes">

Let $\mathcal{D}$ be a passage set over companies $a \in \mathcal{A}$, and let each passage be a
bag of token embeddings on the unit sphere $S^{d-1}$.

**Local.** A query $q$ has a gold company $y(q) \in \mathcal{A}$, and some passage
$d \in \mathcal{D}$ is *about* $y(q)$. An arm answers with the company owning the passage it ranks
first.

**Global.** A query is a theme $\theta$. Each entity $e$ either discusses $\theta$ or does not,
written $m_\theta(e) \in \{0,1\}$, and the gold answer is the community with the most discussants,

$$
y(\theta) = \arg\max_{s} \sum_{e \in \mathcal{C}_s} m_\theta(e),
$$

which is constructed to differ from the nearest community $\arg\max_s \langle \mu_s, \theta \rangle$
for every $\theta$.

</TheoremBlock>

## The corpus, and why its query classes separate

The local corpus needs five query classes, one per mechanism we want to exhibit, and the classes
have to separate for reasons we can name. Our first attempt did not, and the reason is worth
recording because it is a trap anyone building an ablation walks into.

We describe each company by a *triple of latent themes*, one per leg window, and let a query name
those themes with greater or lesser precision. The natural knob is the query's concentration: a
sharply named query should be easy, a vaguely named one hard. Measured, that knob does almost
nothing. What actually determines whether a single view can identify a company is *how many other
companies share that view's theme* — leg accuracy runs from 1.00 when the theme is unique to about
0.50 when six companies share it, and is essentially independent of how sharply the query names it.
Two query classes differing only in concentration are therefore one mechanism sampled twice.

So the sharing structure is constructed rather than drawn, and the query classes are defined by it.
Two families of company, interleaved two per sector so that family does not correlate with sector:

- **Distinctive** companies hold a dense-view theme that *no other company holds*. One view
  identifies them outright. Their other two themes come from the shared pools, so they are generic
  in every other view.
- **Conjunctive** companies hold only shared themes. Every view alone returns a handful of rivals,
  and only the company holding all three themes is the answer.

That single change converts a tuning problem into a construction, and it is what makes the five
classes — `on`, `partial`, `off`, `noisy`, `bridge` — mechanisms rather than difficulty levels.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="A bug the construction was hiding">

The three legs are supposed to read *disjoint* token windows, so that they are partial views of one
document rather than three noisy copies of one score. In our first build the late-interaction
window overlapped the other two — and because a passage is filled window by window, the late draw
silently overwrote half the dense window. The dense leg was reading a mixture of two themes.

The effect was not noise, it was a phantom result: a passage of the gold company matched its query
on *both* mixed components while a rival sharing only the dense theme matched on one, so the dense
leg scored a perfect 1.000 on the very class built to defeat it. The docstring claimed the windows
were disjoint and nothing checked. They now partition the bag, and the partition is asserted at
import.

</TheoremBlock>

## Movement 1 — fusion is set intersection

The hybrid arm fuses three retrieval legs by reciprocal rank, using the rule unchanged from its
own topic: a document scores $\sum_{\ell} 1/(c + r_\ell(d))$ over the ranks $r_\ell$ it receives
from each leg $\ell$.

What can that buy? Only disagreement. If all three legs return the same ordering, the sum is a
monotone function of one rank and fusion returns that ordering unchanged. Fusion contributes
exactly where the views differ, and the shape of the contribution is set intersection.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={2} title="Fusion contributes only where the views disagree">

Let each leg $\ell$ rank documents by a score depending only on theme slot $\ell$, so that leg
$\ell$ cannot distinguish two companies sharing $\theta_\ell$. Write
$R_\ell(q) = \{a : \theta_\ell(a) = \theta_\ell(q)\}$ for the companies leg $\ell$ regards as
candidates.

Then no leg can resolve better than its own $R_\ell$, while reciprocal-rank fusion — which rewards
a document ranked well by *several* legs — concentrates on $\bigcap_\ell R_\ell$. When the triples
are unique that intersection is a single company, even though each $R_\ell$ has several members.

Conversely, if some leg $\ell^\ast$ has $|R_{\ell^\ast}| = 1$ while the others do not, fusion adds
two uninformative rankings to one informative one. The reciprocal-rank sum is symmetric in the
legs and has no way to tell which was which, so the informed leg can be outvoted.

</TheoremBlock>

Both halves are measured, and both directions hold. On the conjunctive class the three legs score
0.05, 0.35 and 0.05 on their own — every one of them returns rivals — while their fusion scores
**1.000**, a margin of 0.65 over the best leg. On the class where one view identifies the company
outright, the single view scores 1.000 and fusion scores **0.800**. Hybrid is not a free upgrade
over naive; it is a trade, and the thing traded away is precisely the case naive was built for.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="Fusion needs different content, not different noise">

Getting the winning half to reproduce took three constructions, and the two failures are kept in
the module as controls.

Drawing all of a passage's tokens around one company prototype makes the three windows three
*subsamples of one signal* — a quality ladder in partial-view clothing — and fusion scored 0.350
against a best leg of 0.400. Blurring the prototype three independent ways gives the legs different
*noise* on the same content; across twenty-three swept configurations fusion never robustly won,
the single positive cell being one query's worth of margin.

Only when each leg's window carries a different *theme* — so that a leg alone retrieves everything
sharing that theme, and only the company sharing all of them is the answer — does fusion gain, and
then it gains everywhere. The condition is that the views carry different content, not that they
carry different errors.

</TheoremBlock>

## Movement 2 — rank against reach

The corrective arm retrieves cheaply, grades what came back, and on a bad grade looks deeper and
scores harder: it over-fetches a shortlist and re-scores it with MaxSim over every token rather
than the single pooled window the cheap leg used.

This is a cascade, and a cascade can only reorder what it fetched. That gives correction a sharp
and checkable boundary.

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={3} title="Correction moves rank; it cannot create reach">

Let the cheap retriever produce a ranking and let the corrective stage re-score its top $k$. If the
gold company's best passage has rank $r$, then correction can promote it only if $r < k$, whatever
the re-scoring is. If no passage in $\mathcal{D}$ is about the gold company at all, no $k$
suffices: the shortlist contains more of the same wrong thing, and a better scorer ranks it more
confidently.

</TheoremBlock>

The two classes we need sit either side of that line, by construction. On the noisy class the gold
sits at median rank 12 — demoted by decoys but present — and correction recovers it. On the bridge
class the gold sits at median rank 23 and is not there in any useful sense at all, because the
company is *named* by a filing rather than described by one. Corrective scores 0.400 on the first
and **0.000** on the second, and so does everything else that scores passages, including full
MaxSim over every token of every document in the corpus.

The part worth dwelling on is that the grader cannot warn us. It is a relevance judge, and a good
one — re-scoring the top retrieved document properly, at a measured AUC of 0.829 against *was the
cheap answer right*. On the bridge class it grades **0.714**. On the class the cheap arm answers
perfectly it grades **0.710**. The two are indistinguishable, and they should be: on the bridge
class the retrieved filing *is* genuinely, highly relevant — it is the filing of the company the
query describes. Relevance is not correctness when the answer is a company no passage is about, and
no amount of calibrating a relevance grader will make it notice.

## Movement 3 — generation corrects position, not content

The HyDE arm throws the query's position away. Rather than retrieving with the query, it generates
a document that would answer the query and retrieves with that, which is why HyDE is indifferent to
how far off the document manifold the query sat.

The mechanism is worth stating carefully, because it is easy to implement an oracle by accident.
Our generator never sees the gold. It conditions on the query, and its one move is to drop the
component along the corpus-mean direction $g$ — the axis that separates queries from documents and
that, by construction, says nothing about *which* company. The de-shifted query is

$$
\tilde q = \frac{q - \langle q, g \rangle\, g}{\lVert q - \langle q, g \rangle\, g \rVert},
$$

and the generator drafts a passage for the company nearest $\tilde q$, or — at a Bernoulli rate
$p$ — for the rival it most easily confuses with that company. A rate rather than a continuous
tilt: a tilt produces a step rather than a floor, because the score gap between the right company
and a confusable one is independent of their similarity and flips at exactly $45^\circ$.

That single move is both the whole of HyDE's correction and the whole of its limitation. A query
tilted *off the manifold* is restored, because $g$ carries the tilt and nothing else: the arm
scores **0.800** where naive scores 0.100. A query tilted *toward the wrong company* is not
restored, because that tilt is not along $g$ and removing $g$ leaves it untouched: the arm scores
**0.000** on the noisy class, exactly where naive does. HyDE fixes where a query is pointing in
representation space. It has nothing to say about a query pointing confidently at the wrong thing.

The collapse anchor is the interpolation weight: $\alpha = 0$ in the imported Rocchio update
returns the bare query, so HyDE with no weight on its generated document is not merely as accurate
as the naive arm, it produces the identical ranking.

## Movement 4 — only a second hop reaches what is named

A bridge passage is a filing of company $x$ that names company $y$. On the mention geometry its
window is $\cos\alpha\,\theta_\ell(x) + \sin\alpha\,\theta_\ell(y)$, so the named company survives
only in the component *orthogonal to the filing it sits in*. Ranking passages by similarity to a
query about $x$ reads the $\cos\alpha$ part and discards the rest, which is why every passage-scoring
arm scores zero on this class.

The agentic arm reads it. It retrieves, selects a filing that opens a direction the query did not
already have, applies the reformulation operator imported unchanged from multi-hop retrieval,

$$
q' = \frac{d - \langle d, q\rangle\, q}{\lVert d - \langle d, q\rangle\, q \rVert},
$$

and retrieves again with $q'$, which now points at $\theta_\ell(y)$. It scores **0.850** where
every other arm scores 0.000.

Two constants had to be measured rather than inherited, and the brief predicted as much. The
mention angle is one: the mention must stay near enough to its own filing to be retrieved at all
($\cos\alpha$) while leaving a residual large enough to reformulate off ($\sin\alpha$). At
$45^\circ$ the bridge is read on only 40% of queries; $35^\circ$ is the measured optimum. The
residual threshold is the other — the multi-hop topic set 0.47 at dimension 32, and here an
ordinary filing measures $0.359 \pm 0.017$ against a bridge's $0.588 \pm 0.090$, so the gap sits
lower and the threshold moved to 0.42.

A third constraint only appeared when we ran it. The bridge source company must be *distinctive*.
A mention sits at $\cos 35^\circ = 0.819$ from the filing it is recorded in, so it is outranked by
anything nearer than that — and a company sharing $x$'s dense theme sits at cosine 1.0. Off a
conjunctive source the bridge passage lands at median rank 24 and no second hop ever reads it.

<TheoremBlock type="remark" title="What the hop costs on every other query">

The agentic arm is the only one that reaches the bridge answer and the **worst** arm overall on the
same regime, at 0.280 against naive's 0.290. That is not a tuning failure, it is the mechanism's
price, and it has a precise cause.

The stopping rule asks whether the filing just read opens a direction the query did not have. It
cannot distinguish a filing that *names a new company* from one that is simply off-target — both
look like a large residual. Worse, the corpus contains a genuine confound: other companies' filings
that mention the gold rank just below the gold's own passages and have large residuals, so on the
class naive answers perfectly the arm reads one of them, follows the link away, and answers with
the mentioning company. It hops 2.7 times on average on that class.

Nothing in the retrieval signal says a second hop is needed. On a bridge query the top-scoring
passage is the gold source company's own filing, confidently retrieved; the query looks answered.
That is why compositional retrieval needs a signal from the *question* rather than from the
results, and it is the honest boundary of a purely retrieval-side controller.

</TheoremBlock>

## Movement 5 — aggregation is precomputed or paid for

The GraphRAG arm is the only one holding a representation of a *set*. It detects communities once,
offline, and each community summary records which of its members discuss a theme. Answering is then
counting over that summary, which is not an operation any ranking of entities performs.

It takes the global regime outright: **1.000**, against 0.062 or less for every flat arm — every
one of which does *worse than guessing* among five sectors, because the corpus is built so that the
nearest sector is never the discussing one.

The honest form of that result is a cost, not an impossibility, and the topic would be dishonest
without it. Flat retrieval *can* reach the same answer: scan far enough down the ranking, keep the
entities that actually discuss the theme, and let those vote. That filter is the aggregation the
community summary holds, performed by hand at query time. The curve is measured —

| entities scanned | 3 | 5 | 8 | 10 | 14 | 20 | 25 |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| accuracy by hand | 0.000 | 0.000 | 0.188 | 0.188 | 0.312 | 0.688 | **1.000** |

— and it reaches the answer at depth 25, which is *the entire entity set*. GraphRAG answers at depth
zero because it paid for the same aggregation once, offline. The claim it earns is not that flat
retrieval cannot get there. It is that flat retrieval can only get there by going deeper and paying
more, which is exactly the trade an architecture is supposed to be.

Read the columns, not the third decimals: twenty queries per class means one query is 0.05, so the
0.10 separating corrective from hybrid on the noisy class is two queries and we decline to call it
a ranking.

What the table says is that there is no dominant row. The arm that takes the global regime is
mid-table locally and tied with the baseline. The arm that owns the bridge class is the worst arm
locally overall. The baseline is unimprovable on one class and last on three. Every arm is best at
exactly one thing, and the thing it is best at is a property of the corpus we can name in a
sentence — which is what turns six names on a cheat sheet into six bets with stated odds.

Costs are reported in two currencies and never collapsed into one, because the exchange rate
between a similarity operation and a generator call is precisely what a cost model should not
invent. HyDE is the cheapest arm in operations and the only local arm that cannot run without a
generator at all. Leaving that as two numbers rather than one is what leaves a Pareto set rather
than a winner — and the frontier over that set is a topic of its own.

## What the average hides

We went in expecting to show that over-eager correction is a net loss. It is not, on this corpus,
and reporting that is more useful than fixing it.

Even with the grader firing on every query and a corrective scorer degraded by noise until it is
barely better than chance at reordering, the corrective arm still scores 0.400 against the
baseline's 0.290. The reason is unglamorous: the baseline is weak enough that a badly reordered
shortlist of twenty is an improvement *on average*.

The damage is real, and it is invisible in that average. As the corrective scorer degrades, the one
class the baseline answered perfectly falls from 1.00 to **0.80**, monotonically, while the overall
number stays comfortably above the baseline throughout. Correction strictly destroys the class that
needed no correcting, and the mean conceals it.

This is the aggregation trap in its purest form, and it is the same trap the *best for* column sets.
An architecture evaluated on a mixture reports what the mixture rewards. Change the mixture — ship
to users who ask more of the easy kind of question — and a change that measured as an improvement
becomes a regression, with nothing in the evaluation having moved.

<FinanceCaseStudy title="Which pattern for which question about a filing" modality={["text", "pdf", "news"]}>

The five query classes are recognizable if you have built retrieval over filings.

*What was this company's rate exposure* names a company with a distinctive business — one view
finds it, and every mechanism above dense retrieval is cost. *A mid-cap energy firm with a pipeline
segment and a trading desk* names no single distinguishing feature; each phrase returns a handful of
candidates and only their conjunction identifies one, which is fusion's case exactly. *What did they
say about supply chains* is written in question-voice rather than filing-voice, which is a
representation mismatch and HyDE's case. *Rate exposure at the regional banks* drags each view
toward a different peer, which is where a grader plus a deeper look pays.

And *which company does this filing's counterparty disclosure point to* is the one that breaks the
others. The answer is a company named in a document that is about somebody else. No re-ranking of
what was retrieved reaches it, no over-fetch depth reaches it, and the retrieval looks perfectly
healthy while it fails — the filing you got back is exactly the filing you asked for.

The five query classes, the two company families, the mention angle of $35^\circ$, the

the sampling error on a cell is about 0.11, so a 0.05 gap is one query.

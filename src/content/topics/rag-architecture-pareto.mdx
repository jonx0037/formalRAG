## The question that has no answer

The predecessor to this topic ran six named RAG architectures as arms over two regimes and found
that no row won both. It also did something quieter that mattered more: it reported cost in **two
currencies** — similarity operations and generator calls — and refused to collapse them into one,
on the grounds that the exchange rate between them is precisely what a cost model should not
invent.

That refusal is what leaves us here, with a Pareto set rather than a winner, and the obvious next
question. *Which one should we deploy?*

The answer is that the question, as asked, has none. It acquires one only after three further
things are fixed, and none of the three is a property of any architecture:

- the **workload mixture** $w$ — what fraction of your traffic is each kind of question;
- the **price ratio** $\rho$ — what one generator call costs in units of a similarity operation;
- the **amortization horizon** $N$ — how many queries an offline index build is spread over.

Move any one and the winner changes. That is not a disclaimer, it is the subject. This topic is
about the *shape* of the dependence: a partition of a simplex, a hull the price slides along, and
a crossover in deployment size.

## Movement 1 — cost is a pair, and it is not a constant

Write $\mathrm{ops}_{a,k}$ and $\mathrm{calls}_{a,k}$ for what arm $a$ spends on a question of
kind $k$. A workload is a probability vector $w$ over the six kinds of question, and an arm's cost
on it is

$$
\mathrm{cost}(a, w) = \Big( \textstyle\sum_k w_k\, \mathrm{ops}_{a,k},\ \ \sum_k w_k\, \mathrm{calls}_{a,k} \Big) \in \mathbb{R}^2 .
$$

Two things about that expression are worth saying slowly.

The first is that it lands in $\mathbb{R}^2$, and $\mathbb{R}^2$ has no total order. "Cheaper" is
no longer a comparison two architectures can always settle. An arm spending a hundred operations
and no generation is not more or less expensive than one spending twenty-five and four generator
calls; the two are simply incomparable until somebody supplies a price.

The second is that $\mathrm{ops}_{a,k}$ genuinely depends on $k$ for some arms, and this is where
the familiar "cost" column of a comparison table stops being merely imprecise and becomes wrong.
Adaptivity turns out to be a spectrum rather than a property, and the spread across question kinds
measures where on it an arm sits. Four of these six are fixed pipelines that do the same work
whatever they are asked, at exactly $1.00\times$. A fifth, the agentic arm, is *nearly* flat at
$1.11\times$ — it stops hopping early when a filing it reads opens no new direction, so its cost is
query-gated too, just barely. Only the corrective arm is adaptive enough for a single cost figure
to be actively misleading rather than merely imprecise: its grader fires on some questions and not
others, and its cost **varies by a factor of 9.95** across the kinds of question it might meet.

So the cost of an adaptive architecture is a function of the workload it meets. There is no number
to put in the column.

## Movement 2 — domination, and what it is a property of

<TheoremBlock type="definition" number={1} title="Domination on a workload">

Arm $a$ **dominates** arm $b$ on workload $w$ when it is at least as good on every axis and
strictly better on one:

$$
q_a(w) \ge q_b(w), \quad \mathrm{ops}_a(w) \le \mathrm{ops}_b(w), \quad \mathrm{calls}_a(w) \le \mathrm{calls}_b(w),
$$

with at least one inequality strict. The **Pareto set** is the set of arms no arm dominates. Note
that both the relation and the set carry $w$ — they are not properties of the architectures.

</TheoremBlock>

On the mixture the predecessor implicitly reported at — its local regime, every class equally
likely — the Pareto set is `hybrid`, `hyde`, `corrective`, `graph`. Two arms are outside it, and
one of those two is outside it comprehensively. The agentic arm is beaten by the naive baseline on
**every axis at once**: lower quality (0.280 against 0.290), more operations (288 against 100), and
more generator calls (2.88 against 0). It is beaten by the graph arm as well.

A reader who stopped there would conclude, correctly by the evidence in front of them, that this
architecture should never be deployed. And then:

<TheoremBlock type="proposition" number={2} title="Domination does not survive a change of mixture">

Let $a$ be dominated by $b$ on workload $w$. It does not follow that $a$ is dominated by $b$ on
workload $w'$, and in general it is false — because both sides of every inequality in Definition 1
are linear functionals of $w$, and a linear functional's sign is not preserved when its argument
moves.

Concretely: `agentic` is dominated on the aggregate local workload, and on a workload consisting
only of the bridge questions it was built for it is the unique arm scoring above zero. The
crossover is measured — it becomes the arm to deploy once the **bridge fraction reaches 0.43**.

</TheoremBlock>

This is the topic's central claim and it is worth resisting the urge to soften it. The aggregate
numbers were not wrong. They were computed correctly, on a mixture, and they support a conclusion
that a different mixture reverses. Domination is a property of an architecture *and* a workload,
and reporting it as a property of an architecture is a category error that a Pareto table makes
very easy to commit.

## Movement 3 — the winner map partitions the simplex

Fixing a price $\rho$ for generation and a price $\lambda$ for cost in units of quality gives a
selection rule, the one every cost-aware evaluation computes:

$$
\hat a(w) = \arg\max_a \Big\{ q_a(w) - \lambda\big(\mathrm{ops}_a(w) + \rho\, \mathrm{calls}_a(w)\big) \Big\}.
$$

The map $w \mapsto \hat a(w)$ partitions the workload simplex into cells. What the partition looks
like is the whole content of "which architecture should we use", and on this arm set it is
genuinely contested: at the headline price all five arms that ever win hold real territory —
`corrective` 68%, `graph` 14%, `hybrid` 9%, `agentic` 6%, `hyde` 3% of randomly drawn workloads.

Raising $\lambda$ collapses the map onto the cheapest arm; lowering it collapses the map onto the
most accurate one. Both limits are degenerate and neither is wrong, which is the uncomfortable
part: **a benchmark's cost weighting decides its verdict before any measurement is taken.** The
weighting is usually chosen once, early, by whoever set up the harness, and is rarely reported at
all.

## Movement 4 — what no price can buy

There is a tempting response to all of this: sweep the prices. If the answer depends on $\lambda$
and $\rho$, vary them, and collect every architecture that wins somewhere. That set — the options
a cost-weighted evaluation can *ever* recommend — is the honest output.

It is not, and the reason is geometry rather than retrieval.

<TheoremBlock type="theorem" number={3} title="Linear scalarization reaches only the hull's vertices">

Fix $\rho$, and write each arm as the point $(c_a, q_a)$ with $c_a = \mathrm{ops}_a + \rho\,
\mathrm{calls}_a$. For every $\lambda \ge 0$,

$$
\arg\max_a\ \big\{ q_a - \lambda c_a \big\} \in \operatorname{vert} \operatorname{conv} \big\{ (c_a, q_a) \big\},
$$

because a linear functional on a finite point set attains its maximum at an extreme point of the
convex hull. Consequently an arm that is Pareto-optimal but lies in the *interior* of the hull is
returned by **no** $\lambda$ whatsoever, and sweeping $\rho$ as well does not help: the argument
holds for each $\rho$ separately.

Such arms are the **unsupported efficient solutions** of multicriteria optimization, and they exist
exactly when the efficient set fails to be convex.

</TheoremBlock>

The practical content is that a Pareto-optimal architecture can be unrecommendable by any weighting
of the objectives — not because the weights were tuned badly, but because no weights exist. It is
dominated by no single arm, and beaten at every price by a *mixture* of two others it sits between.

How often does that bite? Here, **on 22.4% of randomly drawn workloads**, and the arm that goes
missing most often is `agentic` — the same arm the aggregate numbers already dominated. Its second
and third most frequent companions are `hyde` and `corrective`, which is to say: the arms that
spend the second currency.

That convergence is the topic's sharpest point. Both of the standard ways of picking a winner —
compute aggregate domination, or sweep a weighted objective — fail on the *same* architecture, and
for the same underlying reason. It buys a capability that is worthless on most traffic and
indispensable on a little of it, and neither an average nor a linear price can represent that.

## Movement 5 — and the frontier moves under you anyway

One free parameter remains, and it is the one most often left out of a cost model entirely.

The graph arm holds an index nobody else builds: the entity–entity similarity matrix its community
detection runs on, which is $K(K-1)/2$ similarity evaluations. Charged against a deployment of $N$
queries, its cost per query is $\mathrm{ops}_{\text{online}} + B/N$. Below the crossover the build
has not paid for itself, and the domination reverses — at this corpus size the **crossover at
N\* = 3.33** queries.

That number is small, and the honest reading is not that amortization does not matter but that it
does not matter *at this scale*. The scaling is the result worth carrying:

| entities $K$ | 25 | 100 | 500 | 2000 |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| crossover $N^*$ | 3 | 13 | 64 | 253 |

The build is quadratic in the entity count while the saving is only linear, so the deployment must
be **bigger** before the index pays for itself as the corpus grows — the opposite of the intuition
that an index matters more the more there is to index.

## What this leaves you with

Three free parameters, none of them a property of any architecture, and a decision that each of
them moves. That is a worse situation than a ranking and a more accurate one, and it has a
practical residue:

- A cost column with one number per architecture is wrong for any adaptive architecture, and
  wrong by a factor of ten for one of these.
- A Pareto verdict is void the moment the traffic mixture changes, which it does continuously in
  any deployed system.
- Sweeping the weights does not fix it: a quarter of workloads here contain a Pareto-optimal
  architecture that no weighting can return.
- And whether the arm with an index beats the arm without one is a question about how many queries
  you expect to serve.

The useful output of an architecture comparison is therefore not a name. It is the partition —
which regions of workload space each architecture owns, and how far your traffic sits from a
boundary.

<FinanceCaseStudy title="Choosing a stack for a filings assistant" modality={["text", "pdf", "news"]}>

Suppose the assistant answers questions about filings, and you must pick one retrieval stack.

If the traffic is mostly *what did this company say about X* — distinctive companies, one view
sufficient — the cheap arm is already correct and everything above it is cost. If it is mostly
descriptions that match several companies and identify one only in conjunction, fusion earns its
threefold operation count. If a meaningful slice asks *who does this counterparty disclosure point
to*, the iterative arm stops being an indulgence and becomes the only thing that works at all —
and its aggregate numbers will have told you, right up to that moment, that it was the worst option
available.

The price ratio is where this becomes concrete rather than theoretical. A stack built around
generated hypothetical documents spends four generator calls per query. Whether that is cheap
depends on a vendor's per-token price against your own hardware, and on nothing else — and at one
end of that range it is the arm to deploy while at the other it is priced out entirely. It is worth
knowing which side of that boundary you are on, and worth knowing that no amount of retrieval
evaluation will tell you.

</FinanceCaseStudy>

scalarization returns a vertex of the convex hull, so a Pareto-optimal option in the hull's
interior is selected by no weights whatsoever — that is convex geometry, not a fact about
retrieval. And domination is defined relative to a workload, so a change of mixture can create or
destroy it; the witness here is measured but the possibility is structural.

Everything else is a measurement inherited from the predecessor topic and is exactly as synthetic

to fail. The decimals — a 22.4% gap rate, a bridge crossover at 0.43, a five-way split of the

is a mean over twenty queries, so the cell boundaries in the winner map are soft, and the topic
asserts that each boundary exists rather than where it lies.

claim is about the shape of the dependence on all three, never about where any of them sits.
